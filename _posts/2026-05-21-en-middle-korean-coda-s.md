It was once doubted by many scholars that syllable-final (coda) \<s> (ㅅ) in the Middle Korean of the era when Hangul was created was pronounced as a fricative, i.e. \[s],  but it is today generally accepted.[^1]
In this post I take up the question of *when* syllable-final \<s> (ㅅ) began to be pronounced as \[t̚], as it is today.

[^1]: A few scholars, such as Yi Dong-seok (2024), still disagree, but their arguments are largely rebutted in Ko Kwang-mo (2012).

According to Ko Kwang-mo (2023), the change dates from the late 15th century. His reasoning runs as follows:

1. Spellings such as *skuzu-* (< *kuzu-* "to drag"), '*stih-*' (< *tih-* "to crush"), and '*spih-*' (< *pih-* "to sprinkle") that appear in the late 15th century show that \<*sk-*, *st-*, *sp-*> (ㅺ, ㅼ, ㅽ) of this period were spellings not of phonetic s-clusters but of tensed consonants (된소리).
2. In the same period, spellings such as the honorific dative marker -*skuy* (ᄭᅴ) / -*s.kuy* (ㅅ긔) and *ke.li.ski-* (거리ᄭᅵ-) / *ke.lis.ki-* (거릿기-) "to hinder" appear in free variation -- alternate spellings of the same pronunciation. Since \<*-sk-*> (ㅺ) at this time was a tensed-consonant spelling, \<*-s.k-*> (ㅅㄱ) must likewise have been a tensed-consonant spelling.
3. Therefore, by the late 15th century, coda \<*s*> (ㅅ) was no longer pronounced \[s]; it merely represented \[t̚] or the tensing of the following consonant.

There is, however, a hole in this reasoning. It tacitly assumes that the phoneme called "tensed consonant" was realized in Middle Korean in the same way as in Modern Korean. The notion of "tensed consonant" (된소리; also 경음<sub>硬音</sub>, 농음<sub>濃音</sub>, *fortis*, *tense*, *hard*, etc.) is not used only in discussions of the Modern Korean sound system; similar concepts appear in discussions of many of the world's languages. But the way "tensed consonants" are realized differs somewhat from language to language — indeed, even within Modern Korean, their realization subtly varies by environment.

Since Modern Korean cannot be treated as the same language as Middle Korean, one must first establish what exactly "tensed consonant" means in the Middle Korean context — that is, what the phonetic realization of the Middle Korean "tensed consonant" was.
Ko Kwang-mo (2023) offers no such argument, but given the context in which the paper declares that "tensed consonants are voiceless unaspirated sounds," he appears to regard the Middle Korean tensed (plosive) consonants as phonemes realized, as in Modern Korean, only as pure voiceless unaspirated plosives with no fricative component.

That is, although Ko (2023) never states this directly, he seems to hold that word-initial \<*sk-, st-, sp-*> (ㅺ, ㅼ, ㅽ) were realized from the 16th century onward exclusively as \[k˭, t˭, p˭].
Yet this is a fact that is, in practice, impossible to establish from Hangul materials and internal reconstruction alone.

Suppose that \[s] ceased to be pronounced in the phonetic realization of word-initial /sk-/.
The pronunciation of word-initial /sk-/ changed, but it remained a tensed consonant distinct from the plain series, so its opposition to word-initial /k-/ was by and large preserved.
Since /kk-/ could not occur word-initially in 15th-century Korean,[^2] no pre-existing phonemic opposition was lost, and no new phonemic opposition was created.
Only the allophones through which /sk-/ could be realized at the phonetic level changed; at the phonemic level, nothing changed at all.
Had a pre-existing phonemic opposition been lost, we would be able to date the change through the mutual confusion of the spellings of the two merged phonemes.
But because the change of /sk-/ into a "tensed consonant" was not a change of that kind, it cannot be detected through spelling.

[^2]: It could occur word-medially, e.g. the tensing of /k/ after the adnominal ending *-l* in forms like *holkka* (< *ho-l=ka*).

As an exception, spellings like *skuzu-*, *stih-*, and *spih-*, which had already undergone "expressive reinforcement" (표현적 경음화) in the 15th century written with <*sk*, *st*, *sp*> (ㅺ, ㅼ, ㅽ), do give us partial information about the realization of word-initial /sk-, st-, sp-/. But this does not allow us to conclude that word-initial /sk-, st-, sp-/ were *not* realized as \[sk, st, sp] at the time those spellings appear -- a single phoneme can have multiple allophones. Might it not be possible that /sk-, st-, sp-/ were pronounced \[sk, st, sp] in slow or careful speech and \[k˭, t˭, p˭] at ordinary speech tempo?

### What the transcription materials show

How, then, can we learn about the phonetic realization of /sk-, st-, sp-/ from the 16th century onward? 
The obvious answer is: through foreigners' transcriptions of Korean.
There are materials in which foreigners recorded the sounds of contemporary Korean in their own scripts (kana and the Roman alphabet).
Foreign transcriptions of Korean are very useful for us because they preserve information about allophones that cannot be recovered from the Hangul spellings of contemporary Korean speakers alone.

And the behavior of /s/ in these foreign transcription materials directly contradicts the account in Ko (2023).

The Japanese materials are more abundant.
In the *Sōnkai Tōkai Nikki* (尊海渡海日記, 1539), left by a monk who visited Korea in the 16th century, the pronunciations of Korean place names written in Chinese characters are noted alongside in katakana; the following spelling stands out:

* 豆毛浦 - ツム**ス**カイ *tumu****su****kai* (cf. Korean 두못개 *twu.****mos****.kay*)[^3][^4]

Later, in the early 17th century after the Imjin War, works were compiled recording Korean vocabulary and expressions collected from Japanese who had taken part in the war, such as the *Koma-kotoba-no-koto* (高麗詞之事) contained in the *Intokuki* (陰德記). Among them, the following spellings stand out (there are more than a few; I show four representative examples here):

* (13) 女子: スタリ ***su****tari* (cf. MK ᄯᆞᆯ *stol* "daughter")
* (182) 草: スコル ***su****koru* (cf. MK ᄭᅩᆯ *skol* "grass")
* (250) 屎: ストン ***su****toN* (cf. MK ᄯᅩᆼ *stwong* "excrement")
* (160) 衣脫ケ: ヲスハシケラ *o****su*** *pa****si****kera* (cf. MK 옷 밧겨라 *os paskyela* "Take off their clothes!")

[^4]: 두못개 *twumoskay* is a place name on Jeju.

Previous scholarship has raised the possibility that the spelling of s-clusters and coda /-s/ in these materials was "influenced by Hangul orthography," but I consider this possibility very low.
Suppose we accept that claim: Eibokken, in order to faithfully reflect Hangul spelling, wrote a phonetically nonexistent \[s] with Roman \<s> for tensed consonants like /sk-/ and /st-/, even though no \[s] was actually pronounced.
But look at how /n-/ is spelled in forms like *Deuije* "four" (cf. MK *ney*), *Doen* "the eye" (cf. MK *nwun*), *Ana* "one" (cf. MK *hona*): word-initially it appears as \<*d-*>, while medially and finally it appears as \<*-n*>. This reflects the denasalization of word-initial nasals in Korean.
If he followed Hangul orthography so faithfully as to invent nonexistent sounds for the tensed consonants, how do we explain why he did *not* faithfully follow Hangul orthography in the case of /n-/?

Even Lee Ki-mun, who supported the "tensed consonant" interpretation of the s-clusters, said the following about the foreign transcription materials (quoted from Lee 1988):

> Even so, the fact that this material records the 'ㅅ' of the s-initial cluster spellings as *su* cannot be dismissed as insignificant. The possibility that this spelling was influenced by Hangul orthography cannot be entirely excluded, but for now we must proceed on the premise that no such influence existed.

One might try to explain this contradiction between the received theory and the transcription materials as dialectal difference: in the central dialect the \[sC] realization of the tensed consonants had already disappeared by the late 15th–16th century, but it survived in some dialects into the 17th century, and the transcription materials above reflect those dialects.
Since there are records of Eibokken staying in the Suncheon area for a time, his list may reflect the South Jeolla (Suncheon) dialect, and the *Koma-kotoba-no-koto* may reflect the Gyeongsang dialect, the region where the Imjin War was fiercest.
Even granting this, it would mean that the \[sC] pronunciation survived into the 17th century over quite a wide area (South Jeolla and Gyeongsang).
Was the central dialect of the time a "linguistic island," surrounded for some 150 years by \[sC] pronunciations while alone pronouncing tensed consonants without \[s]?
Not impossible, but I am inclined to consider it unlikely.

Moreover, spellings reflecting "expressive reinforcement" with s-clusters, like 'ᄭᅳᅀᅳ-', also appear in the mid-16th-century \<Letters excavated from the tomb of Lady Kim of Suncheon> (순천김씨묘 출토언간):

* 계오 **ᄭᅳ여** ᄃᆞᆫ니니 민망〃ᄒᆞ여 ᄒᆞ노라 \[Suncheon Kim letters]  
*KyeyGwo* ***skuy-e*** *tonni-ni minmang-minmang-ho-ye ho-nwola.*

The example above is unmistakably a 16th-century conjugated form of *skuzGi-* "to be dragged" with the *-zG-* deleted. If, in the Suncheon dialect reflected in Eibokken's word list, word-initial /sk-/ was realized *only* as \[sk], one cannot help asking how, in that same mid-16th-century Suncheon, a form that had undergone "expressive reinforcement" could possibly be spelled with \<*sk-*>.

### Word-initial /sC-/ with two allophones

Unless we abandon the assumption that /sk-, st-, sp-/ each had only a single phonetic realization in the 16th–17th centuries, the contradictions raised above cannot be resolved.
The appearance of *skuzu-*, *stih-*, *spih-*, etc. in the 16th century is no guarantee that /sk-, st-, sp-/ were *always* realized without a fricative in the 16th century.
I grant that *skuzu-*, *stih-*, and *spih-* are likely spellings of "expressively reinforced" forms without phonetic \[s]; but that only shows that word-initial /sk-, st-, sp-/ *could optionally* be realized without \[s] in the 16th century.

As the *Koma-kotoba-no-koto* and the 17th-century Dutch transcriptions show, it cannot be denied that the s-clusters were in some cases still pronounced \[sC] into the mid-to-late 17th century. I therefore draw the following conclusion.

In the 16th century, the word-initial s-clusters ㅺ, ㅼ, ㅽ; the word-initial ㅂ/ㅄ-clusters ㅳ, ㅴ, ㅵ; and the ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ that had undergone "optional reinforcement" (unreflected in spelling until the mid-15th century) were in the process of merging into newly emergent word-initial tensed phonemes /ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ/.[^5] But these /ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ/ could still optionally be realized as the allophones \[sk, st, sp] into the mid-to-late 17th century. That is, word-initially, \[k˭, t˭, p˭] and \[sk, st, sp] stood in free variation.

[^5]: I write the phonemes as /ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ/ for convenience, but they are not different in meaning from the /ㅺ, ㅼ, ㅽ/ mentioned above.

In the Modern Thai spoken in Bangkok, the word-initial phoneme /kr-/ has largely merged with /k-/, but depending on register, words that historically had /kr-/ are sometimes pronounced [kr-], and hypercorrect [kr-] pronunciations are even observed in words that etymologically had /k-/, not /kr/. One might view word-initial /ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ/ in 16th–17th-century Korean as having been in a state similar to Modern Bangkok Thai.

### A new interpretation of syllable-final 'ㅅ > ㄷ'

To support his argument, Ko (2023) investigated whether forms that had medial coda 'ㅅ' in the 15th century, such as '갓가', came to be spelled '갇가' from the 16th century onward, as his account predicts.
But he found that such spellings appear only in the 17th century, not in the 16th.[^6]
Ko (2023) explains this away as orthographic conservatism and treats it as unimportant, but I raise a question here.

[^6]: Kim Seong-ok (2018a, 2018b) found similar facts. Investigating the spelling of stem-final consonants of '옷[衣]', '못[不]', '얻-[得]', '듣-[聽]', '믿-[信]', '받-[受]', etc. in vernacular letters, he reported that there is not a single 16th-century case of '것' or '옷' spelled '걷' or '옫', while '얻-', '듣-', '믿-', '받-' spelled '엇-', '듯-', '밋-', '밧-' occur 0, 1, 2, and 4 times respectively.<br/>This analysis is flawed, however: the 2 cases of '믿-' spelled '밋-' turn out in context to be conjugated forms of '및-[及]'; the 1 alleged case of '듣-' spelled '듯-' could not be verified; and of the 4 cases of '받-' spelled '밧-', one is a conjugated form of '밧-[脫衣]' and another is too unclear in its decipherment to determine. Only 2 examples of '받-' spelled '밧-' remain: "ᄒᆞ나 **밧쟈** ᄒᆞ니 앗가온 ᄃᆞᆺᄒᆞ고" and "ᄎᆞ려 **밧소**" from the Suncheon Kim letters. These are exceptional environments, with endings beginning in 'ㅈ, ㅅ'.

If forms like '갇가' fail to appear in the 16th century because of orthographic conservatism, how were the following changes able to *ignore* orthographic conservatism and appear commonly from as early as the late 15th century?

* (a) ㅅㅁ > ㄴㅁ
  * 구디 지에 ᄒᆞ던 노내 ᄡᅵ **단 말**(< 닷 말) [Sinchang Maeng letters] (1490s)
* (b) ㅅㄴ > ㄴㄴ
  * 우리 논 **인ᄂᆞᆫ** 겨ᄐᆡ셔 경셩 군과니 ᄂᆡ월 열흘ᄭᅴ 드러오ᄂᆞ니 [Sinchang Maeng letters] (1490s)
  * 이 약을 머기면 ᄲᆞᆯ리 효험 **인ᄂᆞ니라** [Changjinbang chwaryo 42a-42b] (1517)
  * ᄉᆞ나ᄒᆡ가 **간나ᄒᆡ가** [Beonyeok Baktongsa I:55b] (1517)
  * 婦人은 사ᄅᆞᄆᆡ게 **존ᄂᆞᆫ** 거시니 [Beonyeok Sohak 3:20a] (1518)
  * 어버ᅀᅵ 다 **인ᄂᆞᆫ** 사ᄅᆞᄆᆞᆫ 그리 호미 므던ᄒᆞ니라 [Beonyeok Sohak 7:23b] (1518)
  * 모미 소사날 길히 **인ᄂᆞᆫ** ᄃᆞᆯ 알에 ᄒᆞ시니라 [Beopjip byeolhaengnok 4b] (1522)
  * 봄 ᄠᅳᆯ헤 어즈러이 춤츄ᄂᆞᆫ 거슨 곳 **ᄎᆞᆫᄂᆞᆫ** 나ᄇᆡ오 [Baengnyeon chohae 90] (1576)
* (c) ㅅㄴ > ㄷㄴ
  * 올히 ᄀᆞᆺ 열여스신 숟**갇나ᄒᆡ**라 [Beonyeok Baktongsa I:45a] (1517)
  * 스스로 그 사오나옴을 다ᄉᆞ리면 나지며 밤의 아직 스스로 **낟낟치** ᄎᆞᆯ화 [Sohak eonhae 5:94b] (1588)<br/>(cf. mid-15th-century '나ᇫ나치, 낫나치' < '낯[箇]-낯-이')
* (d) ㅎㄴ > ㅅㄴ
  * 즐겨 ᄀᆞᆮᄂᆞ녀 즐겨 ᄀᆞᄅᆞ치디 **아닛ᄂᆞ녀**(< 아니ᄒᆞᄂᆞ녀) [Beonyeok Nogeoldae I:6b] (1517)
  * 샤ᇰ녜 아로매 걸일가 **젓ᄂᆞᆫ**(< 젛[恐]-ᄂᆞᆫ) 젼ᄎᆞ로 말ᄉᆞᄆᆞᆯ 조차 업게 ᄒᆞ시니 [Beopjip byeolhaengnok 23a] (1522)

In (a) 'ㅅㅁ > ㄴㅁ' and (b) 'ㅅㄴ > ㄴㄴ', the assimilation of /ㅅ/ to /ㄴ/ before /ㄴ/ is reflected directly in the spelling. In Middle Korean, the sequence /ㄷㄴ/ was assimilated and pronounced \[ㄴㄴ] (cf. 걷\[步]-나\[出]- > 건나-\[濟]); (c) 'ㅅㄴ > ㄷㄴ' can thus be viewed as a kind of hypercorrect spelling arising because /ㅅㄴ/ and /ㄷㄴ/ were realized identically as \[ㄴㄴ]. The sequence /ㅎㄴ/ was also pronounced \[ㄴㄴ] in Middle Korean, and in (d) we can see /ㅎㄴ/ likewise often spelled 'ㅅㄴ'.
On the basis of these spellings, we must conclude that the change 'ㅅ > ㄴ' before nasals had become general by the 1490s at the earliest and the 1510s at the latest.

If reflexes of 'ㅅ > ㄷ' in ordinary syllable-final environments appear only in the 17th century because archaic spelling was used consistently through the 16th century, then it is inexplicable why the pre-nasal environment was exempt from that archaic spelling from as early as the late 15th century.

Previous scholarship has analyzed this 'ㅅ > ㄴ' assimilation as the result of the change 'ㅅ > ㄷ' having already occurred in ordinary syllable-final environments, followed by pre-nasal nasality assimilation 'ㄷ > ㄴ'.
But as we saw above — the transcription of coda 'ㅅ' as \<s> in the \<高麗詞之事> reflecting late-16th-century speech ('*o****su*** *pa****si****kera*' 옷 밧겨라); the fact that spellings like '갓가 > 갇가', '옷 > 옫' begin to appear only in the 17th century; and the transcription in 16th-century materials of the presumably Mongolian food name /tutumas/ (脫脫麻食) as '투투맛, 투투멋' — there is no evidence that coda 'ㅅ' was commonly pronounced \[ㄷ] in ordinary environments through the 16th century; on the contrary, the only evidence there is points the other way.

I therefore propose to view the pre-nasal 'ㅅ > ㄴ' assimilation as a separate change, occurring roughly a century and a half *before* the change 'ㅅ > ㄷ' in ordinary syllable-final environments.
One might wonder how the change 'ㅅ > ㄴ' could occur without passing through 'ㅅ > ㄷ' in ordinary syllable-final environments.
Counterintuitively, however, such a change is not at all remarkable — English shows a similar change.

In the English dialects centered on the American South and African American speech, 'business' is pronounced 'bidness' /bɪdnɪs/ or 'binness' /bɪnnɪs/, and 'isn't' /ɪznt/ and 'wasn't' /wʌznt/ are pronounced 'idn't' /ɪdnt/ and 'wadn't' /wʌdnt/ (Wolfram 2004). Yet these dialects have no general syllable-final change /z > d/; the /z > d/ phenomenon exists only before the nasal /n/. As Wolfram (2004) also notes, this change is easily explained as continuant assimilation:

```
/z/ = [+continuant, -nasal]
/d/ = [-continuant, -nasal]
/n/ = [-continuant, +nasal]
```

That is, the \[-continuant] feature of /n/ spreads to /z/, assimilating it to /d/.

The Middle Korean change is not greatly different.
As shown by spellings like '後ㅿ날' in the Yongbieocheonga and '나ᇫ나치' in the Beophwagyeong eonhae, together with '걷\[步]-나\[出]- > 건나-\[濟]' in the Wolinseokbo, both the voicing of /ㅅ/ to [ㅿ] before nasals and the nasality assimilation /ㄷㄴ/ > \[ㄴㄴ] were already established by the mid-15th century.
Thus /잇ᄂᆞᆫ/ would already have been pronounced roughly \[iznʌn] in the mid-15th century; in the late 15th century it would have undergone the same [z > d] change as in English, yielding [idnʌn], which would immediately have undergone the already established nasality assimilation to be realized as \[innʌn]. We can see that this process requires no 'ㅅ > ㄷ' change in ordinary syllable-final environments.

How, then, did the change 'ㅅ > ㄷ' in ordinary syllable-final environments come about in the early 17th century? Here the (c) spellings 'ㅅㄴ > ㄷㄴ' presented above come to mind. It was because /ㅅㄴ/ > \[ㄴㄴ] and /ㄷㄴ/ > \[ㄴㄴ] were realized identically in the 16th century that /ㅅㄴ/ could also be spelled 'ㄷㄴ' — and if such spellings were possible, then reanalyzing the underlying form of /ㅅㄴ/ as /ㄷㄴ/ was also possible.

Here the spelling '낟낟치' in the Sohak eonhae (1588) draws attention. This word, which continues into the modern standard '낱낱이', appeared in 15th-century Middle Korean as '낫나치', '나ᇫ나치'. Its etymology is clearly the noun '낯\[個]' reduplicated, with the adverb-deriving suffix '-이' attached (낯-낯-이). First, coda 'ㅊ' was neutralized to 'ㅅ' before a consonant, yielding '낫나치';
then, via the pre-ㄴ voicing 'ㅅ > ㅿ' it became '나ᇫ나치', and via the 'ㅿ > ㄴ' process explained above it would have become '난나치'.[^7] '난나치' was originally a reduplicated form (첩어<sub>疊語</sub>), but the finals of the first element '난' and the second element '나치' had come to differ greatly. At this point the author of the \<Sohak eonhae> (1588) reanalyzed the structure of '난나치' as '낟-낟-치' — that is, as a reduplication of a form '낟' that differs from the etymology.[^8] Since the pronunciation of '낟낟치' was phonetically identical to '난나치', this reanalysis could occur easily. And note that the modern reflex of '낯\[個]' is not '낯' but '낱'. In the end, through '낫나치 > 낟낟치', the etymological source word '낯' itself changed to '낱'. This reanalysis was no doubt also aided, coincidentally, by the ㄷ/ㅌ palatalization occurring around the same period.

[^7]: '난나치' does in fact appear in the \<Nobak jimnam> and the \<Beonyeok Baktongsa>.
[^8]: This kind of reanalysis of reduplicated forms appears in other words as well: 'ᄃᆞᆺᄃᆞ시-' /tʌstʌsi/ was reanalyzed as /[s]tʌs-stʌs-i/ and eventually came to be spelled 'ᄯᆞᄯᆞ시'.

Cross-linguistically, a change like word-final /s > t/ is uncommon. In word-final environments, weakening such as s > h is the usual phenomenon; fortition like s > t is hard to find.
To explain this change, Ko (2023) conjectured that Middle Korean word-final /ㅅ/ was pronounced something like [st̚], but there appears to be no convincing reason or evidence that such an allophone existed.[^9]

[^9]: Ko (2023) cites the inclusion of coda ㅅ among the "entering tone" (入聲) codas in the \<Hunminjeongeum Haerye> as one piece of evidence that word-final /ㅅ/ was pronounced [st̚], but there is no problem if the "abruptness" (촉급함) is interpreted with reference to the point at which vocal-fold vibration ceases.

Here I offer one possibility. What if the change '낯 > 낱' via '낫나치 > 난나치 > 낟나치' was not particular to this one word, but rather every Korean word with coda /ㅅ/ was reanalyzed to /ㄷ/ in this fashion? And what if, looking only at the outcome of that reanalysis, we see the 'illusion' of a historical sound change of syllable-final 'ㅅ > ㄷ'? For example: '옷 닙-' [oznip-] > '온 닙-' [onnip-] was reanalyzed with the underlying form '옫 닙-', whereupon '옷' came to be pronounced '옫' in other environments such as '옷 없-' as well.

This may well be a speculation that goes too far. But I place the significance of this discussion in proposing a new direction for investigating the 'ㅅ > ㄷ' change.

### Conclusion

Let us summarize the discussion. Ko Kwang-mo's (2023) account of when the pronunciation of coda 'ㅅ' and the word-initial s-clusters changed collides head-on with the evidence of foreigners' transcriptions of Korean. On the basis of the foreign transcription materials and Hangul spelling patterns, I newly propose the early 17th century as the point at which 'ㅅ' began to be pronounced \[ㄷ] in ordinary syllable-final environments, and I suggest that the realization of the word-initial s-clusters (or the tensed phonemes) may have remained in free variation \[C˭ ~ sC] into the late 17th century. Furthermore, I newly propose that the change 'ㅅ > ㄷ' in ordinary (non-pre-nasal) syllable-final environments may have arisen when the pre-nasal change 'ㅅ > ㄴ' was reanalyzed as an underlying change 'ㅅ > ㄷ' and then extended to other environments.
